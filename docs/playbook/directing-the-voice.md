# Directing the voice — a guide for the editor (you)

*This one is for the human in the loop, not the build tools. The companion's commentary — "Notes
from Andrew" — is the one part of this project that can quietly **undermine** the rest. Everything
else is exact, accurate tooling; the asides are where taste lives, and where AI-drivel sneaks in.
You have the ear for it. This is just the vocabulary, so the thing you already feel as "the ick"
becomes a fast, repeatable call.*

## The one rule

**Craft before character.** The asides are written to a voice standard; *Andrew* is the honest frame
around them (the about page, the section label) — not a character to perform. The moment the prose
starts performing the persona, it goes soft. The fix is always to return to the craft. (This is the
exact mistake we made on *Laudato Si'*: the persona charter crowded out the craft, and the asides
came out glib.)

## The bar — what a good aside does

You can feel all of these in the *Rerum Novarum* and *Magnifica* asides; they are the standard:

- **It resists the easy reading and earns a harder one.** (Not "Babel is optimization gone wrong,"
  but what the text actually claims.)
- **The wit is earned by understanding.** A real idea from inside the text's lineage licenses the
  humor. You can only riff *lovingly* on what you've read closely.
- **Playful about the ideas; reverent about the text.** It humanizes *us*, never pokes the source.
- **It stands alone.** Fascinating even to someone who hasn't read the book — never "this chapter."
- **The last sentence deepens; it doesn't mic-drop.**

## The fast gut-check (your job)

Read the aside and ask one thing: **what did this teach me that the quote alone wouldn't?** If the
honest answer is "nothing — it just sounds good," it's drivel, however nicely it scans. That's the
whole test. Your instinct already runs it; trust it.

## What "the ick" usually is — name it, point at it

When a line feels off it's almost always one of these. You don't need to diagnose *which* — just
quote the line and say "this one." (That's exactly what you did with *"the text is the room,"*
*"survived a budget meeting,"* and *"swap the smokestack for the server farm"* — all dead-on.)

- A **quip** that scores a point instead of illuminating.
- A **cheap swap** — "swap X for Y and the sentence barely changes."
- A **tidy bow** — a short aphorism that snaps the aside shut.
- A **broken metaphor** that falls apart if you look at it (margin / room).
- A **slogan** — a rhythmic, symmetrical clause that *chimes* like insight but only asserts ("heal
  the river while the village stays disposable").
- The **"not X but Y" tic** repeated until it's a drumbeat.
- **Performed humility** — "trust his sentence over mine." Sounds modest, earns nothing. *(The
  automated critic misses this one. You don't.)*

## The division of labor

- **The anti-drivel cook** (the critic loop) clears the floor: it catches most of the above,
  ruthlessly, looping draft → critique → fix until the set passes. Ask for it by name:
  *"run the anti-drivel cook on `<book>`'s asides."* It's defined in
  [critic-panel.md](critic-panel.md).
- **You are the final arbiter.** You've out-caught the critic twice — it rubber-stamps euphonious
  slop because it scans well. The cook makes the set *clean*; you make it *good*. Sign off last.

## The meta-lesson (the whirlwind)

You named it yourself: you nail this when you react to the actual prose, and lose the thread when you
try to systematize mid-flight. So **don't systematize mid-flight.** The examples *are* the system —
when in doubt, reread one *Rerum Novarum* aside, then reread the suspect line; the gap will be
obvious. Flag the specific sentence, hand it back, and let the cook plus a redraft do the rest. The
standard isn't a checklist you hold in your head; it's a quality you recognize. You already do.
